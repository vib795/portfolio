Here’s a confession it took me years of writing software to even notice: I have almost never had to wonder whether my code’s output was correct. Something always told me. The compiler refused. A test went red. A little squiggle appeared under a variable before I’d even hit save. That feedback was so constant, so reliable, that I stopped seeing it, the way you stop noticing the chair holding you up.

Then I started building with language models, and the chair was gone.

Ask a model to summarize a document, or pull a number out of a report, or decide whether a message is angry, and there is no assert waiting to catch a bad answer. The output looks fine. It reads well. It sounds confident. And every so often it is quietly, completely wrong in a way that nothing in your stack will flag. You ship it, and the first thing that tells you it was wrong is a user.

That gap is the entire reason this field exists. If you remember one sentence from this whole guide, make it this one:

Traditional software ships with a compiler that tells you when the output 
is wrong. Language models don't. Almost everything that follows is a way 
to rebuild the feedback loop that missing compiler used to hand you for free.

The rest of this is long, because I wanted it complete. But it’s built on three ideas, and if you hold those three in your head you’ll be able to place any question about this field in about two seconds. Picture a giant cooking competition:

  • Somebody has to taste the food and decide if the dish is actually good. That’s measuring quality, and we call it evals.
  • Before anyone can taste anything, somebody has to write the scorecard and mark the right answers, to decide what “good” even means. That’s labeling, or annotation.
  • And somebody has to run the whole kitchen so hundreds of cooks can work at once without it descending into chaos. That’s the platform.

One opinion I’ll defend the whole way through: the first two layers are how you earn the right to be in the room, but the third is the one that decides everything. A brilliant eval system that nobody chooses to use is a failed eval system. Keep one word in view, because everything lands there in the end: adoption.

Layer 1: measuring quality

Why you can’t just write a test

In normal code, correctness is a fixed point. assert total == 42 means one specific thing, and it means it every single time you run it. Model output takes that comfortable little world and breaks it in three separate places, and each break quietly kills an assumption you didn't know you were leaning on.

It isn’t deterministic. Same prompt, different wording every time, so even the output is a slippery idea. It isn’t binary. “Is this summary faithful to the source?” has no clean true or false, it has degrees, and thoughtful people draw the line in different spots. And usually there’s no reference answer lying around, because nobody wrote down the correct label for a few million examples in advance. If they had, you wouldn’t have needed the model.

Take away determinism, a clean pass/fail, and a reference answer, and the humble assert has nothing left to stand on. That is not a tooling gap you can patch with a nicer library. It's the shape of the problem. Evals are the whole discipline that grew up around living without that compiler.

The eval pyramid

The organizing principle is money. You can’t have a human read every output, and you can’t afford your smartest model grading every output either. So you build a ladder: cheap and constant at the bottom, expensive and rare at the top, and you push every check as far down as it will go.

Think of it like a teacher grading homework in layers. First the instant free checks: did you write your name, is every question answered? Then a fast helper who grades the tricky parts. Then, once in a while, the teacher hand-checks a few to make sure the helper is being fair.

The relationship between the top two rungs is the part I watch teams get backwards, so let me be blunt about it. Humans are the ground truth. The judge is a cheap imitation of the humans. When they disagree, the judge is wrong. Never the other way around. If you ever catch yourself “correcting” a human label because the model disagreed with it, stop and go get a coffee, because something has gone sideways.

And please don’t skip level 1. People do, constantly, because it feels too dumb to bother with. It’s nearly free, it runs on every commit, and it catches an embarrassing number of real regressions before they ever reach the rungs you actually pay for.

Golden datasets

Your eval set is the thing you measure everything against, so where it comes from decides whether any of your numbers mean a thing.

Source it from reality. Sample real production traffic, or realistic stand-ins, and stratify by segment, by difficulty, and by the ways you already know it fails. A golden set made entirely of happy paths only proves the model can handle the cases you were never worried about in the first place.

Start small and grow it from your own mistakes. Fifty to two hundred examples beats zero by a mile, and every production failure becomes a permanent new test case. Here’s the part nobody ever puts on a slide, because it isn’t glamorous: the eval suite is mostly just the residue of every time you sat down and actually read what your system did. Error analysis, which is a fancy name for reading transcripts instead of staring at a dashboard, is where eval criteria are born. You cannot write a good check for a failure you’ve never looked at, and the only way to learn the failures is to look.

Then version it and refresh it. Datasets are versioned artifacts, same as code. Refresh them so they don’t drift away from reality, and keep a held-out slice the team never tunes against, so you can tell real progress apart from quietly memorizing the test.

## The mantra 
Eval the task, not the model. A model's score on some public benchmark tells 
you almost nothing about whether it can do your actual job, whether that's 
answering your customers, pulling fields out of your documents, or moderating 
your community. Public benchmarks are for the people selling models. 
Task-specific evals on your own data are the only ones that predict what 
happens when you ship. I once watched a team pick a model because it topped 
a leaderboard, then discover it was worse at their real task than the cheaper 
one it replaced. The leaderboard wasn't lying. It was answering a completely 
different question than the one they had.

Offline and online, and the loop between them

Offline evals gate deployment. They decide whether a change is allowed out the door. Online signals (A/B tests, user corrections, drift monitors) tell you when your offline suite has quietly gone stale. You need both, plus a loop that feeds the second back into the first.

The classic way this bites you: a change ships green on every eval, and then production complaints start climbing anyway. That’s almost always a coverage gap. Your eval set no longer looks like live traffic, or the team overfit to a frozen golden set. And the fix is not to patch the one incident and move on. It’s to repair the loop. Pull the complained-about cases in as regressions, refresh your sampling from live traffic, keep that held-out slice honest, and stand up monitoring so the gap between offline-green and online-angry gets caught by a dashboard instead of by a person.

The judge: using a model to grade a model

On paper this sounds absurd, and I want to sit in that absurdity for a second because it’s important. You are proposing to use a language model to grade a language model. That’s marking your own homework. Why on earth would anyone trust it?

The honest answer is: you shouldn’t. Right up until you’ve done one unglamorous thing, which is measure how often the judge actually agrees with humans on a shared pile of examples. A judge is a model of human judgment, and it is worth precisely as much as its measured agreement with the humans it’s imitating, and not one drop more. An uncalibrated judge is vibes with extra steps.

Three ways to run one

  • Pointwise with a rubric. Grade one output against explicit criteria. Strongly prefer a plain pass/fail on each specific criterion over a 1-to-10 score. Nobody, human or model, can honestly tell you the difference between a 6 and a 7. The scores pile up around 7 and 8 and stop meaning anything. A binary verdict forces you to write a rubric crisp enough to actually decide.
  • Pairwise. Just ask which of A or B is better. This turns out to be more reliable than absolute scoring, and it’s how the big public model arenas rank things.
  • Reference-based. Compare the output against a known-good answer, on the lucky occasions when you have one.

The judge’s embarrassing little habits

A judge robot has a handful of predictable, almost comical biases. Learning to name them on sight is most of what separates someone who has actually run this from someone who’s only read about it.

Calibration, the step everybody skips

  1. Collect human labels on a sample. A few hundred items is enough to start.
  2. Run the judge on those exact same items, and report agreement with the humans using kappa, not raw percentage. The next section is entirely about why that distinction is not pedantic.
  3. Iterate the judge prompt until agreement clears your bar, then keep watching it, and re-validate the moment the task, the prompt, or the underlying model changes.

That measurement is the technique. The model call is the cheap, easy part. The calibration is the part that makes it real, and it’s exactly the part people skip, because it’s tedious and it involves humans.

There is genuinely good evidence this works. The MT-Bench paper (Zheng et al., 2023) found that GPT-4, used as a judge, agreed with human experts more than 80% of the time, which is roughly how often the human experts agreed with each other. That result is why the whole field takes judges seriously at all. But look closely at the trick hiding inside it: that number was measured on their task. On yours, you start from zero and have to earn it all over again. Which is why “just use an LLM judge” is never the answer, and “calibrate a judge against humans on our task, and keep checking it” always is.

Layer 2: where truth comes from

Judges imitate human labels, so the labels had better be worth imitating. That quietly pushes the whole problem back a step, to a question that sounds trivial and absolutely is not: how do you even know a group of people agree on what the right answer is?

Why raw agreement lies to your face

Picture two friends sorting a big pile of candy into “yummy” and “yucky.” Now suppose almost all of it is yummy. Both friends could be completely lazy, just chanting “yummy, yummy, yummy” without looking, and they’d still match each other on nearly every piece. That match is luck, not agreement. It tells you nothing about whether either of them can actually taste.

Real labeling tasks are that candy pile more often than not, because the interesting class (the fraud, the outage, the policy violation) is usually the rare one. Two people labeling a 95/5 task will agree more than 90% of the time by pure chance, as long as they both mostly guess the common answer. So raw agreement on a lopsided task is a number that flatters you. Chance-corrected agreement is the honest one.

Cohen's kappa:  κ = (p_o − p_e) / (1 − p_e)

  p_o = observed agreement
  p_e = agreement expected by chance

  κ = 1  perfect
  κ = 0  no better than chance
  κ < 0  worse than chance (usually means something is broken)

Read the formula in plain words: of all the agreement that was actually available to earn above dumb luck, what fraction did they earn? Now watch it do its magic trick. Two annotators, 100 items, a simple yes/no label, and in both situations below they land on the same answer 85 times out of 100.

Situation 1 (A says yes 60% of the time, B says yes 55%):
  p_e = (.60 × .55) + (.40 × .45) = .33 + .18 = .51
  κ   = (.85 − .51) / (1 − .51) ≈ 0.69   → substantial, OK to scale

Situation 2 (both say yes 90% of the time):
  p_e = (.90 × .90) + (.10 × .10) = .82
  κ   = (.85 − .82) / (1 − .82) ≈ 0.17   → barely above chance, do NOT scale

Identical raw agreement. Opposite conclusions. One is the kind of number you build a whole pipeline on, the other is noise wearing a nice costume, and the naive metric picks the wrong one every single time on lopsided data. I find this genuinely satisfying, in the way a magic trick is satisfying once you finally see the wires.

There’s a close cousin of this trap worth knowing. Someone proudly tells you their judge agrees with human labels 92% of the time. Sounds great. But if 90% of outputs are “good,” a lazy judge that just says “good” every time also scores 90%, while catching exactly zero of the failures you built it to catch. Report the chance-corrected number, and check agreement on the failure class specifically. A judge exists to catch the bad ones, so measure it where it’s supposed to earn its keep.

Which coefficient, and why there are three of them

If you go looking for kappa, you’ll find not one statistic but a small family, and the differences actually start to matter the moment you run something real instead of a two-person pilot.

Cohen’s is the one in the formula above. Clean, but it assumes exactly two people and assumes both of them labeled everything. Fleiss relaxes the two-rater part. And Krippendorff’s alpha is the one that survives contact with reality, where you have a rotating pool of annotators, nobody labels everything, some items get three sets of eyes and some get one, and your labels might be categories or ratings or spans. It’s the least famous of the three and it’s the one you’ll actually end up using.

The interpretation bands come from a 1977 paper by Landis and Koch, and it’s worth saying out loud that they are a convention, not a law of nature: 0 to 0.20 is slight, 0.21 to 0.40 fair, 0.41 to 0.60 moderate, 0.61 to 0.80 substantial, 0.81 and up almost perfect. In practice a lot of teams won’t scale a task until agreement clears somewhere around 0.67, and hold publish-grade work to 0.8 and up. Don’t treat those cutoffs as sacred. Treat them as “if you’re well under this, stop and figure out why.”

One more habit that saves you: track agreement per class, not just overall. A comfortable-looking overall kappa of 0.75 can be hiding one label sitting at 0.3, and that one class is exactly where your guideline is quietly broken.

Low agreement is a task problem, not a people problem

This is the single most useful sentence I know on the topic, and it will change how you run a team, so I’ll say it plainly. When your annotators come back at kappa 0.35, the reflex is to assume you hired carelessly, or that people aren’t trying. Almost always, that reflex is wrong. Low agreement is your guidelines telling you they’re ambiguous. It means two careful, conscientious people read the exact same instruction and arrived at different, reasonable conclusions, which is a bug in the instruction, not in them.

So the response to a bad kappa is not a stern email. It’s an adjudication session. You put the disagreements up on a screen, you argue them out together, out loud, and you write down why each one resolves the way it does. Those arguments are pure gold, because every one is an edge case your guidelines never covered. You fold them back in, split the two categories that keep getting confused, add a decision tree for the hard calls, and re-run the pilot. The number goes up, not because the people changed, but because the task finally became answerable. And honestly, a pilot that comes back at a suspiciously perfect kappa on the very first try should make you a little nervous, because it usually means the task was trivial, or the annotators quietly agreed on some rule you never actually wrote down.

Annotation at scale

Everything so far is how you measure quality and check that humans even agree. This is the payoff: how you actually produce millions of labels without going broke or lying to yourself about how good they are. Suppose you have a million items and each one needs a sticker. Doing it all by hand would take forever and cost a fortune. Here’s the clever way to do it fast and still get it right, and this one diagram is more or less the entire answer to “design me an annotation platform.”

Stage 1: write the rulebook, then run a tiny practice round

Before anyone sticks a single sticker, you write the rulebook, and especially the tricky parts of it. A usable guideline has a clear definition per label, several positive examples and near-miss negatives (“this looks like X but is actually Y, because…”), a decision tree for the edge cases, and a version number with a changelog. If your annotators have to guess, the kappa will tell on you later.

Then a small practice round, and its mechanics matter more than they look. Two or three annotators label the same 100 to 200 items, because you can only compute agreement on items more than one person labeled, so the pilot needs total overlap. After you scale up, keep 10 to 20% of items double-labeled, so agreement stays a live measurement instead of a one-time ceremony you did once and forgot. Match the statistic to the setup: two raters and complete data is Cohen’s kappa, three-plus raters all labeling everything is Fleiss, and a rotating pool with gaps (which is to say, any real platform) is Krippendorff’s alpha. When agreement comes back low, you already know the move from the last section: adjudicate, fix the task, re-pilot.

Stage 2: let a model do the giant first pass, then route by how sure it is

This is where “LLM-enabled” stops being a slogan and starts earning its name. A model reads all million items and slaps its best-guess label on every one, fast. Now nothing is blank. But those guesses aren’t checked yet, so you route each item by how confident you are the guess is right. Two design questions decide everything here: where confidence comes from, and where you draw the lines.

Get confidence from more than one source, because any single one is weak on its own. The token probabilities of the chosen label are one signal. Self-consistency, where you sample the model several times and use how often it agrees with itself, is another. Ensemble disagreement, where two different model families vote and any disagreement is treated as automatic low confidence, is a strong one. And verbalized confidence, literally asking “how sure are you?”, is a real signal but a badly calibrated one, so never trust it naked and alone.

Then set the thresholds against data, not vibes. Run the pre-labeler on your gold set, bucket the items by their confidence score, and measure the actual precision inside each bucket. Auto-accept only in the buckets where measured precision clears your bar, say 98% or better. The threshold is derived, not guessed.

Here’s the quietly clever bit: routing the uncertain items to humans is active learning, specifically uncertainty sampling. You’re spending scarce human minutes exactly where they buy the most information, instead of scattering them randomly across a million easy calls. Stage 3: keep everyone honest, forever

The gold set is a collection of items with expert-adjudicated, near-certain answers, and the lovely thing is you get it for free as a by-product of your pilot adjudication sessions. Version it. It quietly does three jobs at once: it’s the qualification test for onboarding new annotators, the source of your honeypots (next), and the yardstick you calibrate your model pre-labelers and judges against. Never, ever train or tune on it.

Honeypots are gold items slipped invisibly into every annotator’s queue, around 1 in 20. It’s a surprise pop quiz where you’ve already peeked at the answer key. Because you know the right answer, each annotator earns a continuous, honest accuracy score without anyone having to re-check their work item by item. Keep the honeypots honest themselves: rotate and refresh the pool so they don’t become recognizable, and match their difficulty to real traffic, because all-easy honeypots measure nothing.

A per-annotator dashboard then watches three things: accuracy on the honeypots, agreement with peers on the double-labeled items, and throughput outliers (suspiciously fast usually means “go spot-check this person”). And the response ladder starts gentle: targeted feedback and a guideline refresher first, then down-weighting or re-queuing their labels, then re-qualification. Firing is the last rung, not the first, because, remember, low agreement is usually a task problem.

Finally, watch for drift by trending per-class agreement over weeks, which catches slow guideline rot and annotator fatigue before they poison a whole dataset. Any guideline version bump means re-baselining your honeypot scores. And keep a blind subset, a slice where the model’s guess is never shown, because it’s your only real measurement of the trap that this entire layer secretly revolves around.

The one big trap: don’t just copy the robot

Showing a human the model’s guess before they decide is the exact thing that makes review fast. It is also the exact thing that can quietly hollow that review out until it means nothing. A 2025 study out of MIT (Schroeder et al.) measured it on a subjective labeling task. When people labeled cold, with no suggestion, their labels overlapped with the model about 40% of the time. When they were shown the model’s guess first, overlap jumped to around 85%. The items didn’t change. The people didn’t get smarter. The only thing that changed was whether they saw the machine’s answer before forming their own, and that alone more than doubled how often they ended up agreeing with it.

That’s not random noise. It’s directional, and the direction is straight toward the model. Which means the model’s mistakes don’t get caught, they get laundered. They walk out of your pipeline wearing a “checked by a human” badge, and now they’re sitting in your ground truth, the one place errors were supposed to be impossible. And if you then grade the model against that ground truth, it looks fantastic, because you’re secretly scoring it against labels it wrote itself. You’ve built a closed loop that congratulates itself on its own mistakes.

The fixes are the blind subset (which measures the effect on your task, with your people, for the price of relabeling a small sample the hard way), the honeypots (which catch reviewers who rubber-stamp a known-wrong guess), and one firm rule: never let a single model family generate, pre-label, and judge without a human anchor somewhere in the loop whose entire job is to disagree. None of this means stop pre-labeling. Pre-labeling is what makes labeling at scale affordable in the first place, and uncertainty sampling genuinely puts human attention where it counts. The point is only to stop taking “a human checked it” on faith, and start measuring whether the human is still actually thinking.

Scaling past humans entirely

When the pile is 10 million documents, even routed human review is too slow, and the vocabulary you want is weak supervision. The Snorkel approach is to write many noisy, programmatic labeling functions, then train a label model that learns how accurate each function is and denoises them into probabilistic labels at scale. The economics underneath every one of these decisions fit on a single line: human labels cost dollars, model labels cost fractions of a cent, so the design question is never one-or-the-other, it’s always quality per dollar at a measured error rate.

And pure human labeling stays non-negotiable in exactly three places: your gold and calibration sets, any high-stakes or regulated decision, and anywhere the model’s own failure modes are the very thing you’re trying to measure. You can’t measure a ruler with a copy of itself.

Layer 3: running it as a platform

Everything up to here is how one team measures quality. This is the harder problem, and the one that decides whether you get to lead this kind of work: how does an entire organization measure quality without you personally standing behind every engineer? The mechanics are roughly what you’d guess. The culture is the part that actually determines whether it lives or dies.

Evals as CI. Every prompt change, model swap, and retrieval tweak automatically re-takes the whole test, and a bad delta blocks the merge. Model swaps go through canary evals on the golden set before they’re allowed anywhere near live traffic. “We treat prompts like code” is the entire sentence.

Keep your receipts. Version everything: datasets, prompts, judge prompts, guideline versions, model snapshots. Reproducibility isn’t hygiene, it’s your audit trail. In any setting where the decision can be challenged, whether that’s finance, healthcare, hiring, safety, or just an unhappy customer, “why did the system do that?” needs an answer, and the answer is lineage: from the output, back to the exact model and prompt and dataset versions, back to who or what produced the labels, back to who signed off, back to the eval results that justified shipping that configuration. If any link in that chain is missing, the honest answer becomes “we can’t fully explain it,” which is unacceptable when the stakes are real. That’s why versioning is a feature, not a chore.

Use the cheap robot for the easy stuff. Model tiering, where a cheap model takes first crack and you only escalate to the expensive one when it’s genuinely unsure, often captures most of the savings of a wholesale model swap at almost no quality cost. Add caching, batch APIs for the big offline runs, and sampling for online monitoring. Know rough orders of magnitude, not exact prices. And when someone hands you the classic “design the plan to replace model A with a 10× cheaper model B,” the shape of the answer is: run B through the full regression suite comparing per slice rather than in aggregate (cheap models fail unevenly), put a human-calibrated judge on just the cases where they differ, canary a small slice of live traffic with rollback triggers ready, seriously consider tiering instead of a clean swap, and document the whole decision with versioned results, because that documentation is the audit trail.

Be the slide, not the crossing guard. This is the number one thing that decides whether a platform succeeds, and it’s cultural, not technical. Don’t be the crossing guard who makes everyone stop and wait for permission. Be the playground slide that’s so much fun the other teams line up for it on their own. Concretely: ship SDKs and templates teams pull because it’s genuinely the easiest path, integrate the first flagship team’s workflow for them instead of handing over a doc and wishing them luck, publish the wins, grow a little community of practice around it, and measure adoption (teams onboarded, evals run per week) as a first-class metric. When some team is off running their own janky evals and won’t switch, resist the urge to mandate. Go find out what their setup does that yours doesn’t, because that friction is information, integrate their most painful workflow, and make your road honestly cheaper to walk than theirs. Mandates just breed resentment and shadow tooling. A platform nobody chose to use is a failed platform, no matter how elegant the insides are.

The through-line

If I squeeze this whole guide down to something you could act on tomorrow, it’s this. Stop waiting for the compiler to come back. It isn’t going to. What replaces it isn’t a tool, it’s a stack of habits leaning on each other: read your own outputs and turn the failures you find into cheap deterministic checks; approximate human judgment with a judge you’ve actually calibrated against humans; anchor that judgment in labels whose agreement you’ve measured with the honest statistic instead of the flattering one; produce those labels with a model-assisted pipeline you never stop auditing for the copy-the-robot trap; and wrap the whole thing in versioning and CI so the numbers are reproducible and the easy path is also the right one.

It is genuinely less satisfying than a green checkmark. I miss the green checkmark. But it’s the only thing that actually works once “correct” stops being a fact you can look up and becomes a judgment you have to defend, which, if you’re building with language models, is already the world you live in.
